Binder Sniffer · would it run on a stock phone?

Does It Need Root?

Root isn't needed to hook and decode binder — it's needed to break into someone else's process to do it.

The PoC has two halves, and only one needs privilege. The hook + decoder run inside the target process and touch only its own memory — no privilege. The injector forces a different, already-running app to load that hook — and that is the part a stock device blocks.

As built — No

Injecting into another app

Loading the hook into the testapp from the outside needs root + permissive SELinux. A retail phone gives neither.

The technique — Yes

Hooking your own app

GOT-patch + Parcel decode inside your own process works on a stock device with no root at all.

Why no — the root-dependent parts

Everything that breaks lives in the injector, which uses ptrace to make a different app dlopen our .so. That crosses the app sandbox, and stock Android stops it three ways:

  1. The app sandbox (UID isolation). Every app runs as its own UID. You may ptrace only a process of your own UID or with CAP_SYS_PTRACE. Our injector ran as root; a normal process can't attach to another app at all.
  2. SELinux enforcing. Even as root, production policy forbids ptracing app/system domains. The PoC relied on setenforce 0 (permissive) — which itself needs root and isn't possible on a production user build.
  3. No adb root. On a shipping device adbd runs as shell, not root, so it can't disable SELinux or inject. The emulator gave us adb root and a userdebug/permissive build; a retail phone gives neither.

In other words, the capability the PoC demonstrates — inject into an arbitrary app and read its IPC — is exactly the sandbox-crossing move that root and SELinux exist to prevent. This is why tools like Frida need a rooted device for the same job.

Why yes — the technique isn't the privileged part

To observe your own app's binder traffic, drop the injector entirely:

  1. Load the hook in-process. Build the .so into your own APK and let its __attribute__((constructor)) run at load, or call the GOT-patch from your own code. Patching libbinder's ioctl slot and decoding the Parcel all happen on memory you already own — no ptrace, no SELinux violation, no root.
  2. You're hooking the client side anyway. The testapp is the client sending enqueueNotificationWithTag, so hooking it is a perfect fit. You never needed to reach system_server, which you couldn't touch regardless.

What you still can't do without root: sniff a different app's or the system's notifications. That's the sandbox boundary, and only root (or an exploit) crosses it.

One nuance

A debuggable app (android:debuggable=true) can be reached by shell via run-as without root, because run-as drops to that app's UID. So a debuggable build of your own app is easier to instrument than a release build — but another developer's release app stays closed to you, and ptrace-style .so injection is still awkward there because the shell SELinux domain restricts it. Building the hook into the app is the clean path.

Bottom line: keep the hook in your own process and the whole thing runs on a stock phone; try to reach into another app's process and you're back to needing root.
